# Module 3 — From Aristotle to Newton: Motion
## Big Ideas
- Aristotle developed the earliest theory of motion, which Galileo and Newton later discarded in favor of more powerful ideas — which were themselves eventually superseded by quantum theory and relativity
- Newtonian physics remains useful for macroscopic (everyday-scale) understanding

## Section 1: Aristotelian Physics (A Commonsense View)
- **Natural motion**: Aristotle believed solid objects (made of the element "earth") fall because they're seeking their natural resting place — the center of the Earth. Water/earth fall; fire/air rise — each element seeking its own natural place.
- **Violent motion**: horizontal motion (a cart being pulled, a box being pushed) — required continuous outside influence to keep going, since it goes against an object's "natural" tendency
- **Celestial motion**: the heavens (moon, sun, planets, stars) were made of a special substance called **ether** — weightless, perfect, unchanging — whose natural place was the heavens, moving forever in perfect circles. This is why Aristotle treated heavenly motion as fundamentally different from Earthly motion.
- This model was intuitively plausible (it does *feel* like a push is needed to keep something moving) and explained a lot — which is exactly why it took so long to overturn

## Section 2: Galileo's Objections
- **Two home experiments that break Aristotle's model:**
  - Drop a flat sheet of paper, then the same paper crumpled into a ball — the crumpled ball falls faster, even though it's the *same paper* and should "seek the center" equally either way
  - Drop a feather and a rock together in a vacuum (air removed) — they fall at *exactly* the same rate. In normal air, heavier objects only fall slightly faster than lighter ones of the same shape — and that small difference is entirely explained by **air resistance**, not by "seeking the center" more strongly
- Aristotelian physics did not account for **air resistance** — the resistance to an object's motion caused by its collisions with surrounding air
- **Galileo's Law of Falling**: any two objects dropped together will fall together if air resistance is negligible — true for a cannonball, a frog, or even (as verified in a 1999 experiment) individual atoms
- **Galileo's inclined-plane thought experiment** (how the law of inertia was actually derived): a ball rolling down an incline speeds up; rolling up an incline, it slows down. On a *very slight* incline, this is still true. Galileo imagined removing friction entirely and asked: what happens on a perfectly flat, frictionless surface — the "limiting case" between uphill and downhill? His answer: the ball would neither speed up nor slow down — it would roll forever. This directly contradicts Aristotle (who required continuous force just to sustain horizontal motion) and leads straight into the Law of Inertia (Section 3).
- **Galileo's scientific method** (still the model for how science works today):
  - Design experiments to test specific hypotheses
  - Use idealizations — mentally strip away friction, air resistance, etc., to isolate the main effect
  - Limit the scope — study one variable at a time (Galileo studied horizontal and vertical motion separately)
  - Use quantitative methods — numbers make a theory testable in a way vague description can't
- Galileo was one of the first to use the scientific process as it's understood today — learning from experience and reason rather than authority. (This cost him: his writings on Copernican astronomy led to a Catholic Inquisition trial and house arrest for his final 10 years.)

## Section 3: The Law of Inertia
- **René Descartes** was the first to reason through a *true* zero-gravity thought experiment: imagine no gravity, friction, or air resistance at all. A stone released in midair wouldn't fall — it would just hang there. Flick it, and it would coast in a straight line forever, at unchanging speed. This is deeply counterintuitive because gravity/friction/air resistance are always present in real life, hiding this behavior from us.
- **Law of Inertia**: a body subject to no external influences/forces will stay at rest if it was at rest, and stay in motion in a straight line at unchanging speed if it was already in motion
- **Inertia** = an object's tendency to maintain its state of motion. The word doesn't really *explain* why this happens — it's just the name we give to the fact that it does.
- **Naming note**: this is commonly called "Newton's First Law," even though Descartes came up with it first — Newton just listed it first among his three laws. (Your textbook calls Newton's 2nd law the "Law of Motion" and his 3rd law the "Law of Force Pairs" — worth knowing both naming conventions for exams.)
- You can see inertia in action with anything moving horizontally with very little friction (a bowling ball, an air-hockey puck) — and in outer space, where a spacecraft coasting to the moon is a large-scale demonstration (though even there, gravity from Earth/moon/sun is never fully "zero")

## Section 4: Measuring Motion — Speed & Velocity
- Only two quantities are needed to describe motion: **distance** and **time**
- **Average speed** = overall speed = distance ÷ time → S_avg = d / t
- **Instantaneous speed** = same concept, measured over a much smaller distance/time interval — this is how speedometers work
- **Velocity** is not just speed — it's speed *and* direction
- A formula is just an abbreviation of words — don't be intimidated by them

## Section 5: Acceleration
- **Acceleration** = change in an object's velocity (not just speed) ÷ the time it takes to make that change
- Related to a push or a pull

**Worked sign examples** 
- 10 − 30 = −20
- (−10) − (−30) = +20
- (−30) − (−10) = −20
- (−30) − (+10) = −40

## Section 6: Falling
- Gravity pulls down at roughly **9.8 m/s²**
- Distance fallen (in meters) is proportional to the **square** of the time elapsed

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Air resistance | Resistance to an object's motion from collisions with surrounding air |
| Inertia | An object's tendency to maintain its state of motion |
| Average speed | Distance ÷ time, over the whole trip |
| Instantaneous speed | Speed at a single moment (small distance/time interval) |
| Velocity | Speed + direction |
| Acceleration | Change in velocity ÷ time |
