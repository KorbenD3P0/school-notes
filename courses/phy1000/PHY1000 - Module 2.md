# Module 2 — Atoms: The Nature of Things
## Big Ideas
- The ancient Greeks searched for the underlying unity behind different material substances (wood, gold, water, etc.) — what do they have in common?
- Theories are never "proven," only supported or disproven by evidence — the atomic theory went from an educated guess (Democritus, 5th century BCE) to a well-confirmed theory only after 19th–20th century observations
- Science's understanding of the atom itself has evolved through at least three models: Greek → planetary → quantum — each replacing the last without making it useless within its own proper range

## Section 1: The Greek Atom
- **Democritus** proposed a thought experiment: keep cutting a piece of gold in half — does it go on forever, or hit a limit? He concluded matter must be made of indivisible "a-tomic" ("not divisible") particles, which he called **atoms**
- **The Atomic Theory of Matter**: all matter is made of tiny particles, too small to be seen
- Democritus had only indirect evidence (e.g., you can smell bread from a distance because small particles break off and drift to your nose)
- **John Dalton** (~1800) found the first real evidence: elements always combine in simple weight ratios (e.g., hydrogen and oxygen in water combine at a fixed 1:8 weight ratio by *total* weight) — this is easy to explain if matter is made of atoms. We now know a single hydrogen atom weighs 1/16th of a single oxygen atom, and it takes 2 hydrogen atoms per 1 oxygen atom to make water — which is exactly why the ratio works out to 1:8 by weight.
- Note on terminology: the Greeks' "atom" meant the *smallest possible particle of anything*. Today's "atom" is actually made of smaller parts — electrons, protons, and neutrons (and protons/neutrons are themselves made of quarks). As far as we know today, electrons and quarks are the truly smallest particles — what the Greeks originally meant by "atom."
- **Robert Brown** observed pollen grains suspended in liquid moving erratically for no visible reason (**Brownian motion**)
- **Albert Einstein** (1905) mathematically showed this motion is exactly what you'd expect from countless invisible atoms/molecules constantly colliding with the grains — his predictions matched experiment, cementing the atomic theory
- Theories can be disproven but never fully "proven" — evidence only makes them more or less plausible

## Section 2: Atoms and Molecules
- Chemists can break most substances down into a small set (fewer than 100) of simpler substances that resist further breakdown — these are the **chemical elements**
- An **atom** = the smallest particle of a chemical element
- Elements differ from one another because their atoms differ
- Water is a **compound** of two elements (hydrogen + oxygen) — that's why water can be decomposed but pure hydrogen or oxygen cannot

## Section 3: States of Matter & Thermal Motion
- Nearly every substance can exist in three states: **solid, liquid, gas**
- Molecules are always in random, ceaseless motion — even in solids — and this motion gets faster as the substance gets warmer
- **The Microscopic Interpretation of Warmth**: at the microscopic level, warmth (temperature) *is* the random motion of a substance's molecules — this random molecular motion is called **thermal motion**
- Warmth/temperature (macroscopic) and molecular motion (microscopic) are two views of the same underlying phenomenon

## Section 5: The Incredible Smallness of Atoms
- Atoms are far too small to see with ordinary light — light's wavelength is already 10–100× smaller than the smallest visible dust particle, and an atom is ~5,000× smaller still
- Scientists can't "see" atoms with light, but can detect them with:
  - **Scanning electron microscope** (uses a beam of electrons instead of light)
  - **Scanning tunneling microscope** (1983) — a sharp probe that can even pick up and move individual atoms; in 1990 scientists used one to spell out a lab's name using 35 xenon atoms

## Section 6: Atomic Materialism
- From 1550–1700, Copernicus's ideas spread and fed into the rise of Newtonian physics (motion, force, gravity)
- Newtonian/atomic ideas fed a philosophy called **materialism**: the view that matter (atoms) is the only reality and everything reduces to mechanical motion
- **Democritus's materialist claim**: sensory qualities (sweet, hot, red) aren't truly "real" — only atoms and empty space are real
- **Descartes** distinguished **primary qualities** (real physical properties like weight/size, which science studies) from **secondary qualities** (sensory impressions like "hot" or "red," which he considered products of the mind)
- Materialism is a *philosophical* position, not a scientific fact — science itself can't settle whether atoms are "all there is." Arguments against strict materialism include: science itself starts from sense impressions, not atoms; other worldviews (religious, aesthetic) have equal claim to "reality"; and post-1900 physics shows Newtonian physics (and its atomic materialism) is only approximately correct in a limited range — some things (like light) aren't made of atoms at all

## Section 7: Three Atomic Models
Science's model of the atom itself has changed as evidence accumulated — each new model absorbed and extended the last, rather than fully discarding it:
1. **Greek model** — an atom is a single, unchangeable, solid particle (like a tiny rigid pea). Supported by 19th-century work on elements, compounds, and Brownian motion.
2. **Planetary model** (~1897–1911) — discovery of the **electron** (1897, a particle lighter than an atom) showed atoms have internal parts. In 1911, physicists found atoms are mostly empty space, with nearly all mass concentrated in a tiny central **nucleus**, and electrons orbiting it like planets around the sun. The nucleus itself is made of **protons** and **neutrons**.
3. **Quantum model** (1920s–present) — new experiments contradicted the planetary model (and Newtonian physics itself), requiring **quantum theory** to explain atomic behavior. No experiment has yet disagreed with quantum theory.
- Old models aren't "wrong," just limited in range — the simple Greek atom is still perfectly fine for explaining things like air pressure; you don't need quantum theory for that
- **Theories are best described as useful, not "true."**

## Section 8: Chemistry and Life
- Life = a highly organized "pile of atoms" that can acquire matter, move, respond to stimuli, and reproduce
- Key elements in biological molecules: carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen (mnemonic: **"COHN"**)
- **Antoine Lavoisier** (~1780) carefully weighed materials before/after burning and found no net change in weight — this disproved the old "fire is a substance with weight" theory and helped found modern chemistry
- **Conservation of matter**: the total amount of matter in a chemical reaction is the same before and after (very nearly correct for chemical reactions, though not universally true in all physics)
- Burning combines carbon/hydrogen fuel with oxygen from air to form CO₂ (and H₂O if hydrogen is present), releasing excess thermal energy — e.g., methane: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + excess thermal energy
- **Respiration**: cells react glucose with oxygen to release usable energy, producing CO₂ and water — essentially the reverse of photosynthesis

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Atomic theory of matter | All matter is made of tiny particles, too small to see |
| Chemical element | A substance that cannot be chemically decomposed into simpler substances |
| Compound | A substance made of two or more different atoms bonded together (e.g., water) |
| Brownian motion | The erratic movement of small particles suspended in a liquid/gas, caused by molecular collisions |
| Thermal motion | The random, ceaseless motion of a substance's molecules — the microscopic basis of warmth/temperature |
| Materialism | The philosophical view that matter (atoms) is the only reality |
| Primary/secondary qualities | Descartes's split between real physical properties (primary) and sensory impressions (secondary) |
| Nucleus | The tiny, dense central core of an atom, made of protons and neutrons |
| Conservation of matter | The total amount of matter is unchanged before and after a chemical reaction |
